#### [A] OpenAI — textGrain: Entropy-Calibrated Watermarking for Language Model Text, technical report (2026-10-05)

<https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/e9508624-d767-41b6-a26d-e34ca798ada6/textgrain-entropy-calibrated-watermarking-for-language-model-text.pdf>

- Authors: Xiang Li and Qi Long (University of Pennsylvania), Garrett Wen and Xiaohong Chen (Yale University), and Arzav Jain, Florent Joly, Mike Lam, Qingquan Song and Weijie Su (OpenAI, correspondence to Su).
- It "provides the mathematical details of the textGrain watermark", specifying generation and detection, how to set "a budget for the sampling entropy lost in exchange for a watermark signal", and the assumptions behind the guarantees.
- "The method couples token generation to keyed randomness through an optimal transport problem with costs based on Gumbel random variables and Kullback–Leibler (KL) regularization."
- "The KL divergence from independence equals the reduction in conditional entropy, giving the watermark strength parameter a direct information-theoretic interpretation."
- "To speed up computation, we apply transport to vocabulary blocks, reducing the optimization dimension while preserving relative token probabilities within each block."
- Each position uses a pseudorandom value derived from the secret key and a window of up to `L_ctx` preceding tokens.
- The watermark is unbiased: averaging over keys recovers the next-token prediction (NTP) distribution, but "In practice, a key is often reused across responses".
    - "Unbiasedness alone does not ensure that sampling remains random under that key."
    - Gumbel-max watermarking (Aaronson, 2023) "always selects the same token" at a fixed context and key, so "Repeated generation from the same prompt can therefore produce identical responses".
    - "This matters when an application needs several distinct answers or solutions to the same prompt."
- An entropy budget limits the average loss of sampling entropy to a fraction β ∈ [0, 1] of the NTP entropy, so a coupling "retains at least a fraction 1− β of NTP entropy on average across columns", which "is not a lower bound for every column".
- One generation step (Algorithm 1): a keyed partition of the vocabulary into B blocks, block probabilities summed from the token probabilities after temperature scaling or top-p truncation, a cost table of standardized Gumbel values coupled to m equally likely columns under the budget, a keyed column, then a token sampled inside the chosen block by its original relative probabilities.
- The solver alternates Sinkhorn updates in the log domain with adjustments of the regularization λ towards the target loss (step size a = 1/2), then restores both prescribed marginals with a marginal correction.
    - "A finite iteration limit or numerical tolerance can leave that loss above or below the target, so the requested budget alone is not a guarantee for the numerical output."
    - The cost per position is `O(|A| + N_it·B·m)` for active support A and `N_it` iterations, and solves for several distributions can be batched.
- Detection: "The detector requires only the generated text and the secret key and does not need to know the budget used during generation."
    - It needs "the same tokenizer, block and column counts, context-window rule, and keyed pseudorandom construction as generation", but "Reconstruction requires neither the coupling nor the generating model or entropy budget."
    - The per-position score is `Y_t = −log{1 − F_G(Z_t)}`, the sum over the first occurrence of each distinct context window follows Gamma(n, 1) on unwatermarked text, and a watermark is declared when the sum exceeds the (1 − α) quantile.
    - "A fixed deployed key and finite precision arithmetic require empirical calibration checks; the idealized null calculation does not by itself guarantee the same error rate for every key or application."
- textGrain can be combined with speculative sampling when the draft and target models share the secret key (Appendix B), but "distributional equivalence alone does not establish a speedup", and detection needs no record of draft tokens.
- "Our construction embeds a binary provenance signal during generation", unlike multi-bit watermarks that "can encode identifiers or metadata for attribution".
- The report contains no experiments and gives no deployed values for B, m, β or `L_ctx`.

#### [A] European Commission — Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, text (PDF, published 2026-06-10)

<https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/129555>

- Measure 1.1 asks for at least two layers of machine-readable marking for audio, images, video and containerised text, but "given that free-form text cannot transport metadata, a single-layer of marking as described in Sub-measure 1.1.2 is considered sufficient" for that content.
- Sub-measure 1.1.2: "Signatories will ensure that AI-generated or manipulated content is marked with an imperceptible watermark, with the exception of very short text."
    - "For free-form text longer than 200 tokens, watermarking still needs to be applied, even though it may have lower reliability compared to that of watermarking very long text."
    - "To compensate for the potential lower reliability of watermarking for free-form text, access to the corresponding detection solution may be restricted to verified expert users as detailed in Sub-measure 2.1.2."
- Model providers "are encouraged to implement watermarking at the model level and to enable the smooth integration of the watermarking at the AI system's inference process, to facilitate compliance of downstream providers".
- "Until then, "very short text" should be understood as text shorter than 200 tokens", "with the expectation that this threshold will decrease as new methods become available".
- Free-form text is "A raw sequence of characters with no enclosing structure, schema, or container format", for example "the text that is shown on a website or within a chat".
- Measure 1.2(b) asks signatories to prohibit, in their acceptable use policy, terms or documentation, "the intentional removal of or tampering with metadata markings by deployers or any other third party".
- Sub-measure 2.1.1: the detection solution is free of charge, signatories under 1,000,000 monthly users may charge for very high volumes, and authorities, media, fact-checkers, independent researchers and civil society always get free, unlimited access.

<https://zenn.dev/akari1106/articles/b6d3180cc50dfe>

- The author summarises the mechanism: the base model reads the whole input once (prefill), a small head on top scores every option in parallel, the base weights are frozen, and only the head and LoRA were trained.
- Question types: `noul` returns the probability of yes, `choice` picks one of 2 to 255 options with per-option probabilities and `confidence`, and `score` rates 2 to 10 levels and returns a probability-weighted expected value.
- An API token is created from the dashboard template "Workers AI".
- In the sample response, `is_complaint` came back as noul 0.9266 and `topic` as `returns` at 0.9956 with confidence 0.9869, for 262 input tokens and 0 output tokens.
- Workers AI requires `instructions` for every question, while the Hugging Face card says it can be omitted.
- Output tokens were 0 in all 32 responses checked, and the price table lists only an input price.
- Japanese test: 5 inquiries routed to shipping, returns or billing under three conditions (English/English, Japanese text with English question, Japanese/Japanese) chose the intended team in all 30 calls across both models.
    - Input tokens were 165 to 183, with little difference by language.
- For the deliberately ambiguous inquiry "the refund for a returned item has not reached my account", Clef-flash gave billing 0.777 in English and 0.917 to 0.949 in Japanese, and Clef 0.908 and 0.967 to 0.975.
    - The author wrote the two language versions separately and did not isolate whether wording or the model caused the gap.

    - The published inference code sorts options by option id before passing them to the model.
- Without an `other` option, out-of-scope inquiries (store hours, a job application, brewing coffee) were forced into a team, for example Clef-flash returns 0.454 and billing 0.595.
    - With `other` added, all three went to `other` at 0.961 to 0.997, and in-scope routing barely changed.
    - Without `other`, out-of-scope `confidence` fell to 0.02 to 0.16 against 0.89 or more for in-scope inquiries.
- For inquiries with several requests, `choice` split its probability (which sums to 1) between teams, while one `noul` per team judged each independently.
    - With three requests in one message, Clef-flash missed the shipping request (0.181), while Clef caught it at 0.935.
- Image test with six 512×512 PNG mug illustrations (456 input tokens each): both models caught a wrong colour, even with simulated camera shake (0.972 to 0.989).

- From the published inference code (`joint_schema_model.py`), the author found that the input is ordered as system prompt, images, `state`, then questions and options.
    - When the input exceeds the limit, the end of `state` is cut, and questions and options are never cut, so an input whose questions alone exceed it is an error.
    - Option ids are fed to the model inside JSON such as `{"description":"...","option_id":"returns"}`, so meaningful ids are likely safer (not tested).
    - An object `state` is serialised with `json.dumps(sort_keys=True, ensure_ascii=False)`, so keys are sorted and Japanese is passed unescaped.
    - The system prompt says to decide every field jointly ("Decide every field jointly"), so questions in one request may influence one another.
- Differences between the card and Workers AI: `instructions` optional against required, PIL images against base64 only (no URLs), video supported against absent, and a default `max_length` of 16,384 against 65,536 tokens.
    - The body needs `"model": "clef-flash"` even though the URL names the model.
- Images go as `{ "content_type": "image/png", "base64": "..." }` or as a `data:image/png;base64,...` string, and SVG is rejected.
- Probabilities are rounded to four decimals, and the formula for `confidence` is not published: it falls as probabilities flatten but differs from the top probability.
- The author's conclusion is that the caller's design (an `other` option or a `confidence` threshold, one `noul` per team, `state` ordering for long logs) matters as much as model accuracy, as it did with Jev.

#### [A] Cloudflare Blog — Cloudflare Containers, rebuilt to scale agent sandboxes, by Thomas Gauvin, Rushil Mehra, Gabi Villalonga Simón and Thomas Lefebvre (2026-09-30)

<https://blog.cloudflare.com/faster-agent-sandboxes/>

- Published on September 30, 2026 under the Birthday Week tag.
- Opening: "Today, we're making Cloudflare Containers more programmable and optimized for agent workloads."
- Problem: "Agents don't deploy sandboxes ahead of time."
    - "They create sandboxes on demand, for each task, expect them to be ready immediately, and be able to pause and resume."
- The three changes: "your code can now choose each sandbox's image and instance type at runtime, Containers start 6x faster, and filesystem snapshots are available in public beta."
- Mechanism: "A new scheduling policy moves control over each sandbox into application code, while a redesigned runtime provides a faster path to a running Container."
- "In ComputeSDK's independent benchmark, median startup fell from just over four seconds to 648 milliseconds, and, in our own preliminary tests, burst testing successfully created hundreds of thousands of containers in seconds."
- Every Container gets its own Durable Object, "a persistent, programmable controller running right next to it that manages its lifecycle, outbound traffic, and more".
    - More capabilities move to the native `ctx.container` API "so the Durable Object can control its Container without a wrapper class in between", and the same model is carried into Sandbox SDK 1.0.
- Before: "Until now, Cloudflare Containers has been organized around application deployments."
    - Image and compute resources were chosen at deploy time and rolled out across the application: "The application is the unit of configuration and rollout."
- An agent workspace is "created on demand, while the agent is working", with its image, resources, tools and starting filesystem decided by the task.
    - "It might exist for a few minutes, sleep between requests, or be restored days later."
- Users and integrations named: Base44 (app-building workspaces), Kilo Code (cloud-agent sessions), Cursor Cloud Agents, Devin Outposts, the OpenAI Agents API and Claude Managed Agents.
- Workload needs: coding agents need "repositories, package managers, compilers, test runners, and development servers"; "Evals need sandboxes that begin from a known state"; reinforcement learning systems "need to create, grade, and reset large numbers of environments"; longer tasks need to preserve the files an agent produces.
- The answer is "the `durable_object` scheduling policy", which "lets your code choose each sandbox's image and compute resources at runtime, starts Containers more than 6x faster, and supports filesystem snapshots, so workspaces can be saved and restored."
- Dolev Epshtein, Software Engineer, App Infrastructure at Base44: Containers give "each app an isolated development environment where our AI can execute commands, install dependencies, and bring changes to life in a live preview."
- Emilie Schario, Co-founder of Kilo Code and VP Engineering, AI Workspaces at Anaconda: "every cloud-agent session needs its own workspace and environment, with the right repository, tools, and user configuration."
- Previously each combination of image and instance type was its own Containers application with its own Durable Object namespace, set up ahead of time with `wrangler deploy`.
    - A small Node.js sandbox plus a large Python build sandbox required "two applications, two namespaces, and routing logic in your Worker to send each task to the right one."
- Opt-in configuration in `wrangler.jsonc`: a `containers` entry with `"class_name": "AgentSandbox"`, `"scheduling_policy": "durable_object"` and an `images` map (`node` and `python`, each `{ "dockerfile": ... }`), plus a Durable Object binding named `SANDBOX`.
- Each declared image is available as `this.ctx.container.images.<name>`.
    - The example picks `images.python` or `images.node` from `workspace.toolchain`, `"standard-2"` for builds or `"standard-1"` otherwise, and calls `this.ctx.container.start({ image, instance, enableInternet: true })`.
- "What used to take a separate application and a separate `wrangler deploy` is now an `if` statement."
- "That's the idea behind this whole update: infrastructure becomes code that runs at request time, right down to the environment itself."
- Old rollouts used grace periods, percentage splits and an API call, and "the platform decided which instances got replaced and when, whether an agent was in the middle of a task."
- With the new policy "there's no rollout configuration at all": a Container keeps the image it started with until code stops it, and the next start uses whatever image the code chooses.
    - The example reads a `pinned-image` from `this.ctx.storage`, else uses `images.nodeV2` when `isCanary(this.ctx.id)`, else `images.node`.
- Rollout patterns listed: canary a new toolchain "on 5% of new sandboxes by hashing the Durable Object ID", pin active projects to their current image, migrate a workspace at a checkpoint such as the next session or after a snapshot, and roll back by changing which image future starts choose ("No config push, no waiting for a drain.").
- Old start path: "our global control plane" resolved the application configuration, found capacity and coordinated placement, which "put deployment machinery in the path of an agent's first command."
- New start path: demand begins at the Durable Object, and the infrastructure "looks for capacity on the same machine first, then widens the search within the same location if it needs to."
    - It "favors hosts that already have the Container's image or snapshot in local storage, so the Container can start without downloading it first."
- Runtime: "Instead of booting a new virtual machine from scratch, the new runtime restores a prepared virtual machine that isn't yet assigned."
    - It reuses networking and filesystem setup, batches repeated operations, and "no longer waits on services the first command doesn't need."
- ComputeSDK's Burst TTI Benchmark "launches 100 sandboxes concurrently and measures time-to-interactive from the client".

- "In our preliminary burst test, a single account started 100,000 Containers in 5.387 seconds across six locations."

    - The example starts it with `instance: "standard-2"`, `enableInternet: true` and `entrypoint: ["/bin/sleep", "infinity"]`.
- With it an agent can start a Linux sandbox "without first creating a Dockerfile, building an image, or pushing it to Cloudflare", then use `exec()` "to clone a repository, install packages, and configure the environment for its task."

    - Save with `this.ctx.container.snapshotContainer({ name: "project-ready" })` and `this.ctx.storage.put("workspace-snapshot", snapshot)`; restore with `start({ containerSnapshot: snapshot, instance: "standard-2", enableInternet: true })`.
- Pattern 1: one workspace continues across many sessions, with "The repository, installed dependencies, build caches, configuration, and edits" available "without rebuilding the environment."
- Pattern 2: "Since snapshots are immutable and reusable, multiple Containers can start independently of the same prepared environment and make their own changes from there."

- Pattern: the agent loop runs in the Durable Object, wakes the Container when it needs "a shell, compiler, or development server", and stops that compute while it waits, "paying nothing for idle Linux compute."

#### [A] Cloudflare Docs — Durable Object Container API (2026-09-30)

<https://developers.cloudflare.com/containers/api/durable-object-container/>

- The API is `this.ctx.container` inside any Durable Object class with a container binding, and "The container process runs your image inside a Linux VM."
- `images` (durable_object policy only) is a read-only map from Wrangler's `images` field, where Wrangler "builds or resolves each named image and exposes its digest-pinned reference under the same key."
- `running` is `true` when the container is running, but "It does not confirm that the container is ready to accept requests."
- "`start()` boots a container."
    - "It returns before the container is ready to accept requests."
    - Later errors, including a container that fails to start, surface through `monitor()`, and `start()` throws when the container is already running.
- `start()` options: `env`, `entrypoint`, `enableInternet` (required whenever options are passed), `image`, `instance`, `containerSnapshot` and `labels`.
- `image` takes the managed `cloudflare/debian-trixie` or a value from `ctx.container.images`, and is required unless `containerSnapshot` is passed; the two are mutually exclusive.
- `instance` takes `"lite"`, `"standard-1"`, `"standard-2"`, `"standard-3"`, `"standard-4"` or a custom object with `vcpu`, `memoryMib` and `diskMb`, and "Defaults to `"lite"`."
- `labels` allows up to 10 labels (names 1 to 16 bytes, values up to 64 bytes) that `inspect()` returns.
- Environment variables passed to `start()` do not reach processes started with `exec()`, except `PATH`.
- `exec(cmd, options)` starts the executable directly, without a shell, so Bash must be invoked as `["bash", "-lc", "<COMMAND>"]` or a POSIX shell as `["sh", "-c", "<COMMAND>"]`.
- `exec()` options: `stdin` (a `ReadableStream` or `"pipe"`), `stdout` and `stderr` (`"pipe"`, `"ignore"` or `"combined"`), `cwd`, `env`, `user` (`uid:gid`), `signal` (an `AbortSignal` that sends `SIGKILL`) and `pty` (80 columns by 24 rows when `true`).
- `output()` holds all output in the Durable Object's memory, where it "counts toward the memory limit", so large output should be streamed.
- "`exec()` has no built-in timeout."
- "Canceling the request that started a process does not stop the process."
    - "If the client retries, a second copy of the command runs."
- "`snapshotContainer()` creates a point-in-time snapshot of the writable root filesystem of a running container."
    - "It does not capture memory, running processes, or separately mounted filesystems."
    - "A container started from the snapshot runs its entrypoint again."
- The returned `ContainerSnapshot` is an opaque handle with `id`, `size` in bytes and an optional `name`, and "The snapshot data is not returned to the Worker."
- "Container snapshots are immutable."
    - "Snapshot handles have an implicit 30-day time-to-live that refreshes when you restore them."
- `setInactivityTimeout(durationMs)` keeps a container running after its Durable Object becomes inactive, for more than 0 and at most 6 hours (`21600000`).
    - "A Durable Object that restarts, for example after a deploy, starts without one."
- `getTcpPort(port)` returns a `Fetcher` for HTTP requests or TCP connections to a container port.
- A pending `monitor()` call "prevents eviction until the container exits or for up to 15 minutes, whichever comes first."
- `interceptOutboundHttp()` routes outbound HTTP matching a hostname, hostname glob, IP, IP:port or CIDR range through a `Fetcher`, and an intercept lasts until the container stops.
- With `enableInternet: false` no public resolver answers DNS, and only intercepted hostnames resolve, or every hostname after `interceptAllOutboundHttp()` or a `*` target.
- Intercept limit: 128 entries per container, shared by the three intercept methods, with two entries per hostname (IPv4 and IPv6), so up to 64 hostname targets or 128 IP and CIDR targets.
- "You cannot remove an intercept while the container runs."
- `interceptAllOutboundHttp()` covers port 80 only, and "In a container started with `enableInternet: true`, connections that no intercept covers reach the Internet directly."
- `interceptOutboundHttps()` intercepts port 443 by default, and the container must trust the CA certificate at `/etc/cloudflare/certs/cloudflare-containers-ca.crt`.

#### [A] ComputeSDK — Benchmark Methodology (2026-08-11)

<https://github.com/computesdk/benchmarks/blob/master/METHODOLOGY.md>

- TTI is "The wall-clock time from initiating a sandbox creation request to successfully executing the first command."
- It includes the network round-trip to the provider API, queue time, infrastructure allocation, OS and runtime boot, agent initialization, readiness polling and the first command.
    - It excludes teardown, later commands, file system operations and network speed inside the sandbox.
- Each iteration times `compute.sandbox.create()` plus `sandbox.runCommand('node -v')`, and cleanup is not timed.
- Burst mode: "All sandboxes are created simultaneously — no waiting between launches."
    - It runs 100 sandboxes with a 120-second timeout each, and also reports wall clock, time to first ready and success rate.
- "Why burst matters: AI agents and orchestration tools often spin up many sandboxes at once."
- Runner: GitHub Actions on Namespace runners with Ubuntu and Node.js 24.x, with providers run one after another in random order.
- It does not run from provider-specific regions, use reserved network capacity, or retry failed requests.
- The composite score is the timing score multiplied by the success rate, each metric scoring 100 × (1 − value / 10,000 ms), weighted median 0.60, p95 0.25 and p99 0.15, after trimming the top and bottom 5% of successful iterations.

#### [A] Claude Code Docs — Configure permissions (as fetched on 2026-10-06)

<https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permissions>

- "Deny and ask rules apply when any subcommand matches them, including a command nested inside a subshell, a command substitution, or a control-flow body such as a `for` loop."
- Claude Code strips a leading assignment of certain known-safe environment variables, so `Bash(npm test *)` matches `NODE_ENV=test npm test`, and "An allow rule won't match past an assignment of any other variable."
- "A deny or ask rule matches past any leading assignment, so `Bash(rm *)` in deny still matches `FOO=bar rm -rf tmp/`."
- A Bash rule "covers the invocation Claude usually produces and isn't a security boundary around the program".
- The page's table: `Bash(curl *)` does not stop `/usr/bin/curl https://example.com` or `sh -c 'curl https://example.com'`, `Bash(rm *)` does not stop `/bin/rm -rf build/` or `bash -c 'rm -rf build/'`, and `Bash(git push *)` does not stop `git -C . push origin main`.
- "For filesystem and network enforcement that doesn't depend on the command text, use sandboxing."
- Read and Edit deny rules apply to the built-in file tools, to file commands Claude Code recognizes in Bash (`cat`, `head`, `tail`, `sed`, `tee`) and to redirection targets.
    - They do not apply to `grep -r pattern .` run from the directory that holds the file, or to "arbitrary subprocesses that read or write files indirectly, like a Python or Node script that opens files itself".
- Claude makes "a best-effort attempt" to apply `Read` rules to Grep and Glob, to `@file` mentions and to the selection and open-file context a connected IDE shares.
- Through a symlink, deny rules apply "when either the requested path or the file it resolves to matches", and allow rules only when both match.
- "When a tool then opens the approved file, it confirms that the path still resolves to the location the permission check approved."
- The built-in read-only command set (`ls`, `cat`, `echo`, `pwd`, `head`, `tail`, `grep`, `find`, `wc`, `which`, `diff`, `stat`, `du`, `cd` and read-only `git`) runs without a prompt in every mode and "is not configurable".
- `.claude/settings.local.json` is treated as repository-supplied when it is tracked in git or when `.claude` is a symlink.

#### [A] GitHub, mizchi/skills — formal-methods-reconciler: SKILL.md and references/tool-selection.md (as fetched on 2026-10-06)

<https://github.com/mizchi/skills/tree/main/formal-methods-reconciler>

- Use it "when reconciling software specs, docs, tests, configs, code, logs, or incidents with formal methods", choosing among "Z3, Alloy, TLA+, P, Dafny, MoonBit prove, Lean, Rocq, Why3, Verus, CBMC, Tamarin, or ProVerif".
- "The core stance: the LLM proposes and repairs candidate models; the solver, model checker, verifier, or proof assistant decides; the final result is translated back into domain language for a human decision."
- Step 1 chooses the source of truth: trusted specs, docs, ADRs or API contracts are the expected contract; otherwise code, tests, config and logs are "de-facto behavior, not as automatically correct"; when they disagree, "do not decide alone" but produce a domain question.
- Step 2 extracts claims before choosing a tool (allowed, forbidden, eventually happens, never happens, equivalent, reachable, unreachable, preserves invariant), noting empty, missing, error, timeout, retry and crash behaviour explicitly.
- Step 3 classifies the question as a pure predicate, a relation, a state transition, a message protocol, a sequential code contract, a universal theorem or a security protocol.
- Step 4 picks the smallest tool: "Do not use Lean/Rocq for fast config bug hunting", "Do not use Z3 for temporal interleavings", "Do not use TLA+ for simple predicate consistency".
- Step 5 builds the minimum model without I/O, frameworks, databases or UI, with positive sanity cases to catch a too-strong model and a broken variant "to prove the check is load-bearing".
- Step 6 uses verifier output "as the repair oracle": "Never make a property weaker just to get green unless the domain decision changed."
- Step 7: "A counterexample from the model is a claim about the model, not yet about the code."
    - The agent writes an implementation test that forces the witness (the same input, relation instance, or interleaving driven by barriers, injected crashes or a stopped clock), and a test that fails the same way becomes the regression guard.
    - If the implementation disagrees either way, it is treated "as a model bug first", revisiting assumptions such as isolation level, lock semantics, retry policy or provider guarantees.
- "When the model is small and trusted, keep it as a test oracle: replay model witnesses or model-checker traces as implementation test cases, or diff the implementation against an executable model."
- Steps 8 and 9 translate the result into domain language (who can do what, which order is accepted, which config is dead, which crash sequence loses data) and lock the decision in docs, a CI check or a question to the domain owner.
- The LLM handles claim extraction, tool selection, first-pass formalization, counterexample explanation, repair proposals and wording, but is not "the source of truth for correctness" or "the final judge of a proof".
- The output contract is a formal check with a passing or failing command, a translated witness, a regression guard candidate, a reproducing implementation test, or a ledger entry; "If no formal model is worth building, say why and propose the cheaper check."
- The tool map: Z3 for pure predicates (validators, feature flags, config, eligibility), Alloy for relations (RBAC, tenant isolation, ownership), TLA+ for state transitions (retry, crash, queue, outbox, liveness, all interleavings), P for message protocols, Dafny for sequential code contracts, Lean 4 for universal theorems, and Tamarin or ProVerif for security protocols.
- Splits: "Z3 for input predicates; Alloy for entity/relation worlds", and "Alloy for structural holes; TLA+ when order/retry/crash creates the bug".
- Before modelling, the agent asks itself questions such as "What bad state should be unreachable?", "What witness would convince a domain owner?" and "What command can run this check in CI?"

- Part 3's conclusion: code did the physics, the landing prediction and the search, and with clean input kev-4b read the SAFE/DEATH labels, but in the only job left to it (choosing among SAFE options) it showed no advantage over the one-line rule "prefer right".
    - That rule went from 12 of 20 crossings to 0 of 20 when delayed by kev-4b's response time (144 ms), a delay that inserting a model always adds.
    - The more facts and rules are passed, the longer the input and the staler the screen each decision is based on.
    - The author's slogan is to stop making a System-1 model do work that does not need a System-1 model.
- Even the rightward rule reached the right edge in only 6 of 20 runs: 8 died after landing on the platform (1 by the rising floor, 7 in the pit; 5 did nothing on the platform, 3 of them predicted to stay on it), and 6 timed out (4 because the descending board made all six options DEATH, 2 following a "do nothing" recommendation in the safe band).
- For client work, the author recommends sorting decisions into three kinds before proposing System-1 models:
    - Computable in code (physics, timing, rules), such as landing prediction, trap triggers and search: code.
    - Needing speed but not writable as rules: a System-1 candidate, which this game did not yield.
    - Slow and ambiguous, such as finding a death's cause from a recording: an LLM or a person.
- In the earlier article, fast methods were right only 40% to 80% of the time on business branching; here, in real-time control where speed is essential, the fast model's decisions did not turn into results.
    - The author's current view is that System-1 speed pays off for decisions with ambiguity that rules cannot capture, a tolerance of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and a small cost of error.
- Five acceptance criteria for a PoC:
    - Keep a control without the model, matched on input, constraints, goal and delay, record who decided each decision, and report the model alone separately from the whole system.
    - Run each setting at least three times, since platform landings swung from 31 to 2 on a rerun and from 0 to 23 across the final three runs.
    - Test input limits and where truncation happens, since laya-ml's cut at 48, 192 and 512 tokens dropped the labels.
    - Rerun regression tests after any change of wording or language, since a label's wording moved laya-ml's probability 3.7 times and English made it pick "attack".
    - Compare the latency budget with the speed of the events, measuring screen capture (55 ms here) before the model, since the trap rose in 12 to 14 frames while a decision cycle took 13 to 34.
- Placement multiplies model size by distance: a laya-ml-sized model is fastest next to the screen, and a 4B model like kev-4b is expected to beat the Mac from a GPU in a nearby region (Tokyo unmeasured).
    - The author adds that Azure Machine Learning managed online endpoints face the same GPU quota and region-proximity constraints.
- Limitations the author lists as decisive:
